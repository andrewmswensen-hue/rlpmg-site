<h2 id="three-problems">What Are the Three Most Likely Problems?</h2>
<h3>1. Price</h3>
If a listing has been live for two weeks with zero inquiries, price is the first suspect and usually the guilty one. Renters searching on Zillow or Apartments.com filter by budget, so an overpriced unit doesn't get argued with; it gets scrolled past without ever being seen. The market's verdict arrives as silence.

The instinct to "leave room to negotiate" works against you here. Zillow's research on overpricing in the for-sale market found that listings that linger end up selling at steeper discounts the longer they sit (<a href="https://www.zillow.com/research/overpricing-impacts-time-market-12476/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Zillow Research</a>), and the rental version of that dynamic is vacancy: at $1,500 per month of target rent, each week the unit sits empty costs about $350, which swallows the $50 premium you were holding out for in under two weeks. The math is laid out in <a href="/blog/rental-pricing-vacancy-columbus/">how overpricing creates the vacancy it was supposed to prevent</a>.
<blockquote class="post-pullquote">
<p><strong>Zero inquiries after two weeks isn't a market problem. It's a pricing problem.</strong></p>
</blockquote>
<h3>2. Photos</h3>
Photos are the click. Renters scrolling a results page choose which listings to open based on the first image, and dark, blurry, vertical-phone shots lose that contest to the professionally photographed unit three results down, at the same rent. A listing with four dim photos reads as "something's wrong with this one" even when nothing is.
<h3>3. Description</h3>
A vague description fails twice. It gives renters too little to act on (no square footage, no parking answer, no pet policy), and it leaves out the details platform search filters run on, which means the listing doesn't surface for renters filtering on those features. "Charming 2-bedroom in great location" tells nobody anything.

&nbsp;
<h2 id="quick-fixes">Which Quick Fixes Actually Work?</h2>
Each diagnosis has a direct fix, and all four of these can happen within a week:
<ul>
 	<li><strong>Adjust the price with data, not hope.</strong> Pull current comps for your specific area, not the citywide average. Even a $25–$50/month adjustment can move a listing into the next filter bracket and meaningfully change visibility. A <a href="/free-rent-evaluation/">free rent evaluation</a> gives you the comp-based number rather than a guess.</li>
 	<li><strong>Reshoot the photos.</strong> Natural light, wide angles, tidy rooms, and at least 10 images covering each room plus the exterior. Shoot on a sunny day with the lights on. This is the highest-return afternoon an owner can spend on a vacant unit; the data behind it is in <a href="/blog/rental-listing-photos-vacancy-time/">how professional photos affect lease-up time</a>.</li>
 	<li><strong>Rewrite the description around facts.</strong> Square footage, bedroom and bathroom count, school district, parking, pet policy, included appliances, laundry, and proximity to major employers (a "15 minutes to OSU Wexner Medical Center" line does real work in Columbus). Details are what convert a view into an inquiry.</li>
 	<li><strong>Expand distribution.</strong> A listing that lives on one platform reaches only the renters who search on that platform. RLPM syndicates listings to 45+ rental sites including Zillow, Trulia, and Apartments.com, because renters are spread across all of them and inquiries scale with reach.</li>
</ul>
One more lever owners overlook: the pet policy itself. Zillow's July 2025 analysis of 11+ million listings found pet-friendly rentals rented about eight days sooner and drew 9% more views (<a href="https://zillow.mediaroom.com/2025-07-02-Saying-yes-to-pets-pays-off-for-landlords" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Zillow, 2025</a>). If your listing says "no pets," it's turning away most of the applicant pool before price or photos ever get a vote.
<blockquote class="post-pullquote">
<p><strong>You can't fix a listing you're not monitoring. If you don't know your views and inquiries, you're flying blind.</strong></p>
</blockquote>

<h2 id="call-for-help">When Should You Call for Help?</h2>
Fix the price, refresh the photos, upgrade the description, and give the market another week. <h2 id="faq">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<strong>Why is my rental listing getting views but no applications?</strong>
Views without inquiries usually mean the photos earned the click but the price or description killed the follow-through. Check your rent against current comps first, then make sure the description answers parking, pets, and square footage.

<strong>How long should a rental listing take to get applications in Columbus?</strong>
A well-priced, well-presented listing typically generates inquiries within the first week. Two weeks of silence is a diagnostic signal, not normal market friction. RLPM's average lease-up runs 4–6 weeks once a property is rent-ready.

<strong>Should I lower my rent if nobody is applying?</strong>
If comps confirm you're above market, yes, and sooner beats later: at $1,500/month, a week of vacancy costs about $350, more than most price adjustments. Base the number on comps, not on frustration.

<strong>How many photos should a rental listing have?</strong>
At least 10, covering each room, in natural light with wide angles. The first photo decides whether renters open the listing at all.

<strong>Where should I list my rental property?</strong>
Broadly. Renters search across Zillow, Trulia, Apartments.com, and dozens of smaller sites, so single-platform listings miss most of the audience. RLPM syndicates to 45+ platforms.
