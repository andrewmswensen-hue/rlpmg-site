<aside class="post-box post-tldr"><p class="post-box-label">TL;DR</p>
<p>When a tenant stops paying rent in Ohio, the process runs in stages: communication and documentation in the first days, a formal demand and payment alternatives by mid-month, a statutory 3-day notice if nothing resolves, then an eviction filing in municipal court. An uncontested Franklin County eviction typically takes around six weeks. Most non-payment situations resolve before court, which is exactly why a consistent process matters.</p>

</aside>
<aside class="post-box post-takeaways"><p class="post-box-label">Key Takeaways</p>

<ul>
 	<li>Communication resolves most late-rent situations in the first two weeks; escalation to court is the exception, not the rule (about 3.5% of RLPM-managed tenancies escalate to eviction, litigation, or significant dispute).</li>
 	<li>The 3-day notice required by <a href="https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-1923.04" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ORC 1923.04</a> is a legal prerequisite to filing. Skipping it, or getting the wording wrong, restarts the clock.</li>
 	<li>Franklin County Municipal Court's eviction filing fee is $149 for possession only, as of August 2026, and hearings are typically set 2 to 3 weeks after filing.</li>
 	<li>Columbus's Pay to Stay ordinance (passed July 2023) lets a tenant defeat a non-payment eviction by paying all past-due rent, reasonable late fees, and court costs before judgment.</li>
 	<li>Self-help eviction (changing locks, shutting off utilities) is illegal in Ohio and exposes the owner to damages plus the tenant's attorney fees under <a href="https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-5321.15" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ORC 5321.15</a>.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="day-1-5">Day 1–5: Rent Is Late. Now What?</h2>
The first few days are about communication, not confrontation. If the lease includes a grace period, it runs first. Once it lapses, the late fee specified in the lease applies, and the first contact goes out: a friendly, direct reminder that rent hasn't been received.

Most late payments end here. A forgotten autopay, a paycheck that landed a day late, a bank transfer that bounced: these account for the bulk of late-rent events, and a reminder plus an online payment link resolves them within days. Online rent collection makes a measurable difference at this stage, because it removes the friction of checks and mail from a moment when friction is the enemy. (More on how that works in the guide to <a href="/blog/rent-collection-columbus-property-management/">how Columbus rent collection works with a property manager</a>.)

One habit matters more than any other this week: document each contact. Date, channel, what was said, what was promised. If the situation resolves, the notes cost nothing. If it doesn't, they become the record a magistrate reads.
<blockquote class="post-pullquote">
<p><strong>Most non-payment situations resolve before eviction. The ones that don't are why you need a process.</strong></p>
</blockquote>
&nbsp;
<h2 id="day-5-15">Day 5–15: Still No Payment. What Does Escalation Look Like?</h2>
If a week has passed with no payment and no meaningful response, the tone shifts from reminder to formal. A written late notice or demand letter goes out stating the amount owed, the late fees assessed, and a clear deadline.

This is also the assessment window. Is this a one-time hardship from a tenant with two years of on-time history, or the third late month in a row? The answer shapes the path:
<ul>
 	<li><strong>Payment plan or one-time arrangement.</strong> A structured catch-up schedule, in writing, can rescue a tenancy worth keeping. It costs far less than a turnover.</li>
 	<li><strong>Rental assistance referral.</strong> Franklin County and Columbus agencies administer emergency rental assistance, and Columbus City Code requires landlords to accept rent tendered by a third party on a tenant's behalf (CCC 4551.06, as of August 2026). Connecting a struggling tenant with assistance can make the owner whole without a courtroom.</li>
 	<li><strong>Continued escalation.</strong> If there's no communication and no payment, the file moves toward the statutory notice.</li>
</ul>
RLPM pursues these alternatives deliberately before court, including pay-and-stay arrangements, because an eviction is the most expensive way to solve a rent problem. Practical strategies for this middle stage are covered in <a href="/blog/late-rent-payment-strategies-columbus-property-management/">dealing with late payments: strategies for working with tenants</a>.

<h2 id="three-day-notice">Day 15–30: How Does the 3-Day Notice Work in Ohio?</h2>
If payment isn't made and no arrangement is reached, Ohio law requires a specific step before any eviction can be filed: the 3-day notice to leave the premises, served under <a href="https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-1923.04" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ORC 1923.04</a> (as of August 2026).

Three things about this notice trip up self-managing owners:
<ul>
 	<li><strong>It must be in writing and contain exact statutory language</strong>, displayed conspicuously: "You are being asked to leave the premises. If you do not leave, an eviction action may be initiated against you. If you are in doubt regarding your legal rights and obligations as a tenant, it is recommended that you seek legal assistance." Leave that language out and the notice can be defective.</li>
 	<li><strong>Service has rules.</strong> The notice may be handed to the tenant, left at the residence, or sent by certified mail with return receipt.</li>
 	<li><strong>It is a prerequisite, not a formality.</strong> A court will not hear an eviction that skipped the notice. Getting it wrong means starting over and losing weeks.</li>
</ul>
The notice should state the amount owed. And while the statute counts three days, the practical message to the tenant is simpler: this has become a legal process, and the window to resolve it directly is closing.
<blockquote class="post-pullquote">
<p><strong>The 3-day notice isn't paperwork. It's the legal foundation of the case. Defective notice, defective case.</strong></p>
</blockquote>
One more thing the notice is not: permission to act on your own. Ohio law flatly prohibits self-help. Shutting off utilities, changing locks, removing belongings, or threatening any of it makes the owner liable for the tenant's damages and attorney fees under <a href="https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-5321.15" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ORC 5321.15</a> (as of August 2026). The process is the only lawful path.

<h2 id="filing">Day 30+: What Does Filing an Eviction in Franklin County Involve?</h2>
If the notice period passes without resolution, the next step is filing a forcible entry and detainer action under <a href="https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/chapter-1923" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ORC Chapter 1923</a>. For Columbus-area properties, that means <a href="https://www.fcmcclerk.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Franklin County Municipal Court</a>.

RLPM uses professional local attorneys for court filings rather than sending owners or staff to argue cases, and makes the effort to recover these costs from the resident where possible. Filing for money damages alongside possession raises the filing fee (currently $186 for two causes), which is a case-by-case decision with the attorney.

The timeline from filing runs roughly like this: Franklin County Municipal Court sets the hearing 2 to 3 weeks after filing, depending on service. If the court grants judgment for possession, the tenant typically has a short window before a red tag is posted and a bailiff-supervised set-out can be scheduled. End to end, an uncontested case usually lands around six weeks from notice to possession, per the timelines published by <a href="https://www.ohiolegalhelp.org/eviction-timeline" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Ohio Legal Help</a>. Contested cases, counterclaims, or continuances can stretch the process to months.

<h3>Columbus Pay to Stay: the rule that changes the endgame</h3>
For properties inside Columbus city limits, one local law matters here. Columbus's Pay to Stay ordinance (Ordinance 2109-2023, passed July 2023, creating Columbus City Code 4551.07) gives tenants an affirmative defense to a non-payment eviction: if the tenant tenders all past-due rent plus reasonable late fees before the case is filed (or those amounts plus court costs after filing but before judgment), the eviction fails. The ordinance also treats late fees above the greater of $50 or 10% of monthly rent as unreasonable for this purpose (<a href="https://columbus.legistar.com/LegislationDetail.aspx?ID=6295962&amp;GUID=DE3FD3D2-7AEF-48A9-B7B0-3F13428BEC0B" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Columbus Ordinance 2109-2023</a>, as of August 2026).

For an owner, this is not necessarily bad news. Getting the balance paid was the goal on Day 1. Pay to Stay simply means the legal process can end with payment rather than possession, and a well-documented ledger makes the accounting clean either way. Several suburbs, including Worthington and Gahanna, have adopted their own versions, so the rules depend on where the property sits.

<h2 id="after">After the Eviction: What Comes Next?</h2>
Possession is not the finish line. The weeks after a set-out carry their own workload and their own costs:
<ul>
 	<li><strong>Move-out inspection and deposit processing.</strong> Condition is documented, and the security deposit is applied and itemized within Ohio's 30-day window under <a href="https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-5321.16" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ORC 5321.16</a>. The <a href="/blog/security-deposit-rules-ohio-landlord/">security deposit rules guide</a> covers what can be deducted.</li>
 	<li><strong>Turn scope and re-listing.</strong> The property gets assessed, repaired, and back on the market. Turn quality drives how fast the next tenancy starts.</li>
 	<li><strong>Recovery efforts.</strong> Judgments against former tenants are hard to collect, but RLPM makes the attempt rather than writing the balance off by default.</li>
</ul>
Counting lost rent, the turn, and vacancy, a realistic all-in cost for a non-payment eviction runs $5,000 to $10,000 or more, even when the legal fees stay under $500. That number is the real argument for the early-stage work in this article, and for the screening that happens before a tenancy ever starts. The line-by-line cost breakdown lives in <a href="/blog/eviction-cost-ohio/">how much an eviction costs in Ohio</a>.

Prevention has one more layer worth naming: consistent enforcement of payment terms from month one. Owners who waive late fees casually or let partial payments drift train their tenants to treat the due date as a suggestion. A systematic policy, applied the same way for each resident, is both a Fair Housing safeguard and the cheapest eviction-prevention tool available. How to structure those fees is covered in <a href="/blog/late-rent-fees-ohio-landlord/">late rent fees in Ohio: what you can charge and how to enforce it</a>.

<h2 id="faq">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<strong>How long does an eviction take in Ohio?</strong>
An uncontested non-payment eviction in Franklin County typically takes around six weeks from the 3-day notice to possession. Contested cases can run months longer.

<strong>Can I evict a tenant in Ohio without going to court?</strong>
No. Lockouts, utility shutoffs, and removing belongings are prohibited by <a href="https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-5321.15" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ORC 5321.15</a>, and violations make the owner liable for damages plus attorney fees (as of August 2026).

<strong>How much does it cost to file an eviction in Franklin County?</strong>
The filing fee is $149 for possession only, or $186 with a money-damages claim, per the Franklin County Municipal Court Clerk's fee schedule effective August 2026. Attorney fees for uncontested cases typically add around $100.

<strong>What is Columbus's Pay to Stay ordinance?</strong>
A 2023 Columbus law (CCC 4551.07) that lets a tenant defeat a non-payment eviction by paying all past-due rent and reasonable late fees before filing, or those amounts plus court costs before judgment.

<strong>Does a late payment mean I should start an eviction?</strong>
Usually not. Most late payments resolve with communication, a payment arrangement, or rental assistance within the first two weeks. Eviction is the tool for the cases that don't respond to any of that.

<strong>Can I accept partial rent during an eviction in Ohio?</strong>
Accepting payment during a case can affect it, and inside Columbus a tender of the required amounts can defeat the eviction entirely. Talk to your attorney before accepting anything mid-case.

<strong>What percentage of tenancies end in eviction?</strong>
Across RLPM-managed properties, roughly 1.2% of tenancies escalate to eviction, litigation, or significant dispute. Careful screening up front is the biggest reason that number stays low.
