<h2 id="business-case">Should You Allow Pets in Your Rental? The Business Case</h2>
Start with the size of the market you'd be turning away. The American Pet Products Association counts 95 million U.S. households with a pet (<a href="https://americanpetproducts.org/industry-trends-and-stats" target="_blank" rel="noopener">APPA, 2025 survey</a>), and among renters specifically, Zillow's <a href="https://www.zillow.com/research/renters-housing-trends-report-2024-34387/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">2024 Consumer Housing Trends Report</a> found 58% of renter households have at least one pet, which is more than report having children. The same research found 60% of renters call pet-friendliness a critical factor in choosing a rental.

Meanwhile the supply side hasn't kept up. The Michelson Found Animals <a href="https://www.foundanimals.org/pet-inclusive-housing-trends-report-2025-outlook/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Pet-Inclusive Housing Trends Report (2025)</a> found 72% of renters struggle to find pet-friendly housing, fewer than 11% of rentals in major markets qualify as truly pet-inclusive, and 80% of the properties that do allow pets impose breed restrictions. That mismatch is the opportunity: in a Columbus market where most competing listings say no or say yes with heavy conditions, a truly pet-friendly property stands out before the renter reads a second sentence.

The performance data backs it up. Zillow's <a href="https://zillow.mediaroom.com/2025-07-02-Saying-yes-to-pets-pays-off-for-landlords" target="_blank" rel="noopener">July 2025 analysis of more than 11 million rental listings</a> found pet-friendly rentals were typically snapped up eight days sooner and earned 9% more views, 12% more saves, and 11% more shares than listings that didn't allow pets. Eight days of avoided vacancy on a $1,500 Columbus rental is roughly $400 of preserved income on a single turn. Retention points the same direction: the Michelson research ties pet-inclusive policies to tenants staying up to 21% longer, and pet owners face real switching costs each time they move, because the next pet-friendly unit is hard to find.
<blockquote class="post-pullquote">
<p><strong>Allowing pets isn't a risk. Having a bad pet policy is the risk.</strong></p>
</blockquote>
None of this means accepting any animal on any terms. It means the math favors saying yes with structure over saying no by default.

<h2 id="structure">How Do You Structure a Pet Policy That Protects You?</h2>
A protective pet policy has two halves: the rules that shape which animals move in, and the financial structure that covers you when something goes wrong.
<h3>The rules side</h3>
<ul>
 	<li><strong>Written lease terms.</strong> Breed and weight restrictions, if you use them, belong in the lease, not in a conversation. So does the limit on the number of pets per unit (two is a common ceiling for single-family rentals).</li>
 	<li><strong>Pet screening.</strong> Vaccination records, licensing, and basic behavioral history, collected before approval. In Central Ohio, licensing means the county dog license Ohio requires for dogs three months and older (<a href="https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-955.01" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ORC 955.01</a>, as of August 2026), registered through the county auditor. Third-party pet screening services make the whole intake systematic rather than personal.</li>
 	<li><strong>Documented criteria applied uniformly.</strong> Approving one tenant's 70-pound dog while denying another's, with nothing in writing to distinguish them, is how owners create Fair Housing exposure out of a pet policy.</li>
</ul>
<h3>The financial side</h3>
Ohio leases commonly use some combination of a pet deposit, monthly pet rent, and a pet fee. The structural weakness of a traditional pet deposit is its size: a $300 deposit meets a $2,000 damage problem and loses. That gap is what RLPM's pet program is built to close. Under the Pet Protection Program, an approved pet's damage beyond normal wear and tear, exceeding the security deposit, is covered up to $4,000 (starting 2026), funded by a monthly pet administration fee paid by the resident rather than the owner. The resident pays for the privilege of the pet; the owner gets a backstop several times deeper than a typical deposit. The program mechanics are covered in <a href="/blog/landlord-pet-policy-columbus-ohio/">RL's pet protection policy for property owners</a>.

Whatever structure you use, one habit ties it together: document pet damage separately from ordinary wear at move-out, with photos, so the deduction story is clean. The <a href="/blog/security-deposit-rules-ohio-landlord/">Ohio security deposit rules guide</a> explains why that separation matters in a dispute.
<blockquote class="post-pullquote">
<p><strong>A $300 pet deposit against a $2,000 damage problem isn't a policy. It's a hope.</strong></p>
</blockquote>

<h2 id="assistance-animals">Assistance Animals: What Changed in 2025–2026, and What Didn't</h2>
<p><em>This section is informational, not legal advice. The rules here shifted recently and continue to develop; consult a fair housing attorney before denying or conditioning an accommodation request. Current as of August 2026.</em></p>
Assistance animals sit outside your pet policy entirely. Under the federal Fair Housing Act, a person with a disability can request a reasonable accommodation to keep an animal that assists with that disability, and that request is evaluated under disability law, not under your breed list or pet fee schedule. Under the ADA's narrower definition, service animals are dogs individually trained to perform tasks for a person with a disability (<a href="https://www.ada.gov/resources/service-animals-2010-requirements/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">ADA.gov</a>); the FHA's assistance animal concept has historically been broader, including emotional support animals.

Here's what changed. HUD rescinded its 2020 assistance animal guidance (FHEO-2020-01) in September 2025, and a <a href="https://www.hud.gov/sites/default/files/hudclips/documents/AS-Trainor-Enforcement-Guidance-Assessing-Requests-for-the-use-of-an-animal-as-a-reasonable-accommodation-under-the-fair-housing-act.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">May 2026 HUD enforcement memorandum</a> made the rescission permanent. Under the new posture, HUD's own enforcement focuses on animals individually trained to do work or perform tasks, and HUD no longer treats waiving pet fees for untrained emotional support animals as presumptively required. The background on the earlier shift is covered in <a href="/blog/hud-fair-housing-guidance-2026-ohio-landlord/">what Ohio landlords need to know about HUD's 2026 fair housing guidance changes</a>.

Here's what didn't change, and this is the part that keeps owners out of trouble:
<ul>
 	<li><strong>The Fair Housing Act itself.</strong> The statutory duty to consider reasonable accommodations for people with disabilities is law, not guidance, and rescinding a HUD memo doesn't repeal it.</li>
 	<li><strong>Private lawsuits and case law.</strong> Tenants can still bring FHA claims directly, and years of court decisions recognizing emotional support animal accommodations didn't disappear in 2025.</li>
 	<li><strong>State and local rules.</strong> Ohio civil rights law and local fair housing agencies operate independently of HUD's federal enforcement choices.</li>
</ul>
The practical takeaway for a Columbus owner: treat an assistance animal request as a legal process, not a pet application. Evaluate it individually, document the interaction, and involve an attorney before saying no or attaching fees. A denial that would have been risky in 2024 may still be risky in 2026, just through a different door. RLPM handles accommodation requests through established procedures for exactly this reason, and the screening-side details are covered in <a href="/blog/pet-screening-fair-housing-ohio/">pet screening, service animals, and fair housing compliance</a>.
<blockquote class="post-pullquote">
<p><strong>You can restrict pets. Assistance animals answer to a different rulebook. Knowing the difference is what keeps owners out of court.</strong></p>
</blockquote>

<h2 id="mistakes">What Are the Most Common Pet Policy Mistakes?</h2>
The expensive pet problems in a rental portfolio are rarely caused by the pets. They're caused by policy gaps. The recurring ones:
<ul>
 	<li><strong>A blanket "no pets" stance that also refuses assistance animals.</strong> This is the mistake with legal teeth. "No pets" can be a lawful policy; applying it to a legitimate accommodation request is a fair housing violation waiting to be filed.</li>
 	<li><strong>Inconsistent enforcement.</strong> Allowing one resident's cat off the books while charging another resident pet rent, with no documented criteria, undermines both the lease and your Fair Housing position.</li>
 	<li><strong>Fees specified but not collected.</strong> A pet fee that exists in the lease and not in the ledger sets the precedent that lease terms are optional.</li>
 	<li><strong>No damage documentation at move-out.</strong> Pet damage claimed without photos separating it from normal wear tends to lose in a deposit dispute. The move-in baseline matters just as much; see <a href="/blog/move-in-inspection-columbus-rental-property/">what a move-in inspection should document</a>.</li>
 	<li><strong>Ignoring the unauthorized pet.</strong> Animals appear mid-tenancy. A quarterly inspection cadence surfaces them early, when the fix is a lease addendum and a fee, not a move-out surprise.</li>
</ul>
Each of these has the same fix: decide the policy once, write it down, and let the system enforce it uniformly.

<h2 id="faq">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<strong>Should I allow pets in my Columbus rental property?</strong>
For most owners, yes, with structure. 58% of renters have pets, and pet-friendly listings lease about eight days faster per Zillow's 2025 data. A screening process, lease terms, and damage coverage manage the risk.

<strong>How much pet rent or pet deposit can I charge in Ohio?</strong>
Ohio doesn't cap pet fees or deposits for ordinary pets; the lease controls. Common structures include a one-time fee, monthly pet rent, or a refundable deposit. Assistance animals are a separate legal category.

<strong>Can I restrict dog breeds in my rental?</strong>
For pets, yes, if the restriction is written into the lease and applied uniformly. Check your landlord insurance policy too, since some carriers impose their own breed exclusions.

<strong>Do I have to accept an emotional support animal in 2026?</strong>
The answer got more complicated after HUD rescinded its 2020 guidance (September 2025, made permanent May 2026). HUD enforcement now centers on trained assistance animals, but the Fair Housing Act, private lawsuits, and state law still apply. Get legal advice before denying a request.

<strong>Can I charge pet fees for an assistance animal?</strong>
Under the rescinded 2020 HUD guidance, no. As of the May 2026 HUD memo, fee waivers are presumptively required only for trained assistance animals under HUD's enforcement posture, but courts and state law may see it differently. Have an attorney review before charging.

<strong>What happens if a pet damages my rental beyond the deposit?</strong>
Under RLPM's Pet Protection Program (starting 2026), approved-pet damage beyond normal wear that exceeds the security deposit is covered up to $4,000, funded by the resident's monthly pet administration fee.
